An expense log is a list of what you spent, one line per purchase, written close to the time you spent it. Logging daily keeps each entry to a few seconds; a monthly review turns the lines into totals you can act on. The same method works for household spending and for a business. The difference is that a business log also has to hold up as a tax record, and the tax rules quoted here are US federal (IRS) ones.

## Why daily, and why weekly is the fallback

Memory is the weak link. The IRS makes the point in [Publication 552, Recordkeeping for Individuals](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p552.pdf) (last revised January 2011): "You may forget an expense unless you record it when it occurs." For businesses, [Publication 583, Starting a Business and Keeping Records](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583), says: "Generally, it is best to record transactions on a daily basis."

If daily doesn't stick, weekly is the floor. For travel, meal and car records, [Publication 463, Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) (2025 edition), says you don't need to write everything down the same day:

> You don't need to write down the elements of every expense on the day of the expense. If you maintain a log on a weekly basis that accounts for use during the week, the log is considered a timely kept record.

Past a week, entries turn into reconstruction from card statements, and cash purchases are the first to go missing.

## What to write for each entry

Keep the entry short enough that you will actually make it. These fields cover a household budget and a business log alike:

| Field | Example | Why it's there |
|---|---|---|
| Date | 3/14/2026 | Puts the entry in the right month, and the right tax year |
| Merchant | Harbor Market | Lets you match the line to a card statement or receipt |
| Amount | 47.82 | The total you paid, tax included |
| Category | Groceries | Drives the monthly totals |
| Paid with | Debit ending 0937, or Cash | Tells you which statement to check; cash has no statement |
| Note | Weekly shop | What it was for, if the merchant name doesn't say |
| Receipt | Yes, in March folder | Where the receipt is, or that there wasn't one |

For a personal budget, date, amount and category are the minimum. For a business, the IRS page [What kind of records should I keep](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/what-kind-of-records-should-i-keep) says supporting documents should identify "the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date incurred," and include a description showing the amount was for a business expense. The table's columns cover each of those.

You don't have to copy everything off the receipt. Publication 463 says: "You don't have to record information in your account book or other record that duplicates information shown on a receipt as long as your records and receipts complement each other in an orderly manner." A log line that points to the receipt, plus the receipt, is enough.

## The routine: daily, weekly, monthly

**Each day (about a minute).** Enter each purchase when you make it, or all of them before bed. Write the note while you still remember what the purchase was for. Put paper receipts in one place, or scan them; see [scanning receipts on iPhone](/guides/receipt-scanner-iphone/).

**Each week (about ten minutes).**

1. Open your card and bank activity for the week.
2. Check that every charge has a log entry, and add any you missed.
3. Add cash purchases from memory or from the receipts in your wallet. Publication 583 says that if you can't get a receipt for a cash payment, "you should make an adequate explanation in your records at the time of payment."
4. Fix categories that drifted ("Coffee" one day and "Dining out" the next).

**Each month (about twenty minutes).**

1. Total each category for the month.
2. Compare the totals with last month and with any budget you set.
3. Look at the "Other" total. If it's large, split something out into its own category.
4. For a business account, reconcile the statement against your records. Publication 583: "You should reconcile your checking account each month."
5. Note anything you expect next month: an annual subscription, a car registration, an insurance premium.

## A worked month

A household logs every purchase for October. A few days of the daily log:

| Date | Merchant | Amount | Category | Paid with | Note |
|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| 10/1 | Harbor Market | 86.14 | Groceries | Debit 0937 | Weekly shop |
| 10/1 | City Transit | 34.00 | Transport | Visa 4821 | Monthly pass top-up |
| 10/2 | Lucia's Tacos | 23.50 | Dining out | Cash | Lunch with a friend |
| 10/3 | Northside Power | 112.47 | Utilities | Bank transfer | September electricity |
| 10/4 | Streaming service | 15.99 | Subscriptions | Visa 4821 | Renews on the 4th |

At the end of the month the review gives these totals:

| Category | Entries | October total |
|---|---:|---:|
| Groceries | 14 | 412.36 |
| Dining out | 6 | 96.50 |
| Transport | 9 | 138.00 |
| Utilities | 3 | 184.21 |
| Subscriptions | 2 | 32.98 |
| Household | 4 | 57.40 |
| Other | 1 | 18.00 |
| **Total** | **39** | **939.45** |

The useful finding here is small: two subscriptions, one of them unused. That is what a monthly review is for.

If you are self-employed, use the expense lines of Schedule C as your business categories so the year-end totals map straight onto the form; the [self-employed expense tracking guide](/guides/self-employed-expense-tracking/) lists them.

## Categories and separate logs

- **Keep the list short.** Eight to twelve categories is enough for most households. Too many and you hesitate over each entry.
- **Spell each category the same way every time.** A total can only add what it can match.
- **Keep business and personal apart.** Publication 583 says: "You should keep your business account separate from your personal checking account." A separate log, and a card tied to the business account, keeps business totals clean.
- **Split mixed receipts.** One store run with groceries and printer paper is two entries, or one entry with the business part noted. [Publication 334](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334) says to "separate the personal part from the business part."

## Paper journal, spreadsheet or app

| Method | Daily entry | Monthly totals | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper journal (one page per month, ruled into columns) | Quick, if the notebook is with you | Added by hand | Arithmetic errors; one copy |
| Spreadsheet | Slower on a phone, quick on a computer | Formulas do the adding | Rows and receipts drift apart |
| Phone app | Quickest, since the phone is always with you | Calculated as you go | Your data lives in the app, so check how you get it out |

A paper journal works if you carry it. A bullet-journal spread with columns for date, merchant, amount and category is the same log on paper; add the month's category totals on the last page of the month. For the spreadsheet route, [tracking expenses in a spreadsheet](/guides/expense-tracker-spreadsheet/) gives a column layout and the formulas for monthly and category totals.

## When the log needs receipts

For household spending, the log is for you, and receipts matter mainly for returns and warranties. Most personal purchases have no federal tax use; [which receipts to keep for taxes](/guides/which-receipts-to-keep-for-taxes/) covers the exceptions and how long to keep them.

For a business, the log alone is not proof. Publication 583 says supporting documents for an expense "should show the amount paid and that the amount was for a business expense," and that the law generally does not require "any specific kind of records." The log is your summary; the receipts and statements back it up. For filing them, see [how to organize receipts](/guides/how-to-organize-receipts/).
