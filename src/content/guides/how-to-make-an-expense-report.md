An expense report lists business costs you paid yourself and asks whoever owes them, your employer or a client, to pay you back. Your employer's form or expense system decides the layout. What goes into it, and how quickly, comes from the US federal tax rules on reimbursements in [Publication 463, Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463) (2025 edition). This guide covers those rules for employees, then the version for freelancers billing a client. State rules vary.

## What goes in an expense report

Publication 463 says: "Your employer should tell you what method of reimbursement is used and what records you must provide." Ask for the form or the system first. Whatever the format, a report carries the same parts:

| Part | What to enter | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Who and when | Your name, the report period or trip dates, and who approves it | Approval and payroll need to know whose money it is and which period it covers |
| Business purpose | One line for the whole report ("Client workshop, Denver") and a few words per item where it isn't obvious | The IRS treats business purpose as an element you must prove |
| One line per expense | Date, merchant, category, amount | Publication 463 counts each separate payment as a separate expense |
| Receipts | One per line, attached or uploaded, matched by date and amount | Documentary evidence for the amounts |
| Advances | Any cash advance or per diem already paid to you | The report has to account for every dollar you received |
| Total | Expenses minus advances | What you are owed, or what you owe back |

For travel, Publication 463 lists what your records must show in its Table 5-1: the cost of each separate expense, the dates you left and returned, the destination, and the business purpose. An expense report that records those four elements covers what Table 5-1 asks for; the receipts and the accounting for advances complete it.

## The rules behind the deadlines: accountable plans

Many employers reimburse under an accountable plan; ask yours whether it does. Publication 463 says such a plan must include all of these rules:

- "Your expenses must have a business connection—that is, you must have paid or incurred deductible expenses while performing services as an employee of your employer."
- "You must adequately account to your employer for these expenses within a reasonable period of time."
- "You must return any excess reimbursement or allowance within a reasonable period of time."

What counts as adequate accounting is spelled out in the same chapter:

> You adequately account by giving your employer a statement of expense, an account book, a diary, or a similar record in which you entered each expense at or near the time you had it, along with documentary evidence (such as receipts) of your travel, mileage, and other employee business expenses.

It adds that you "must give your employer the same type of records and supporting information that you would have to give to the IRS if the IRS questioned a deduction on your return."

A reasonable period of time depends on the facts, but Publication 463 lists time limits that always count as reasonable:

| Step | Treated as reasonable if it happens |
|---|---|
| You receive an advance | Within 30 days of the time you have the expense |
| You account for your expenses | Within 60 days after they were paid or incurred |
| You return any excess | Within 120 days after the expense was paid or incurred |
| You answer a periodic statement (at least quarterly) asking you to account or return advances | Within 120 days of the statement |

The practical rule: submit the report within 60 days of the expense, and pay back any unused advance within 120.

**Why it matters to you.** If you meet the three rules, Publication 463 says your employer "shouldn't include any reimbursements in your income in box 1 of your Form W-2." Reimbursements that fail the rules are generally treated as paid under a nonaccountable plan, and those are added to your wages in box 1.

**You usually can't deduct it yourself instead.** Publication 463 says: "Form 2106 is only used by Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or local government officials, and employees with impairment-related work expenses." Other employees can't use that form to deduct work expenses. And: "If you are entitled to a reimbursement from your employer but you don't claim it, you can't claim a deduction for the expenses to which that unclaimed reimbursement applies." For most employees, the expense report is the only way to get the money back.

## Per diem instead of receipts for meals

Some employers pay a per diem allowance, which Publication 463 defines as "a fixed amount of daily reimbursement your employer gives you for your lodging and M&IE when you are away from home on business." M&IE is meals and incidental expenses.

A per diem can stand in as proof of the amount, but not of everything. Among Publication 463's conditions, the allowance must be no more than the federal rate, and you must still "prove the time (dates), place, and business purpose of your expenses to your employer." The federal rates are set per city on the [GSA per diem rates page](https://www.gsa.gov/travel/plan-book/per-diem-rates). Many employers use the IRS high-low rates instead, and Publication 463 explains that method. Its 2025 edition prints rates that have since been replaced: for travel on or after October 1, 2026, [Notice 2026-60](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-60.pdf) sets $329 a day for travel to a listed high-cost locality and $230 a day for any other locality in the continental United States, of which $86 and $74 are for M&IE. The notice allows transition rules for the last three months of 2026, so your employer may still be on the earlier rates until December 31, 2026; ask which it uses.

Two points from the same chapter:

- **Unused days go back.** If you don't prove you actually traveled on each day you were paid a per diem for, you must return that amount within a reasonable period.
- **An allowance above the federal rate is partly wages.** In Publication 463's example (a March 2025 trip), an employee gets $90 a day for M&IE in Phoenix, where the federal M&IE rate was $86. The trip is cut from 5 days to 3, so the employee returns $180 for the 2 days not traveled, keeps the $4-a-day difference for the 3 days traveled, and that $12 is reported as wages on Form W-2.

## Freelancers: billing expenses to a client

If you are self-employed and a client agrees to pay your travel or other costs, the report is usually an expense section on your invoice, with the receipts attached. Publication 463's rules for independent contractors:

> If you received a reimbursement or an allowance for travel, or gift expenses that you incurred on behalf of a client, you should provide an adequate accounting of these expenses to your client. If you don't account to your client for these expenses, you must include any reimbursements or allowances in income.

It adds: "You must keep adequate records of these expenses whether or not you account to your client for these expenses." Meals have one more rule. If you don't separately account for meal expenses and seek reimbursement for them from the client, the 50% limit on meals applies to you.

So list expenses as their own invoice lines, separate from your fee, with meals on lines of their own, and keep a copy of every receipt you send. How those costs then appear on Schedule C is covered in [tracking business expenses when you're self-employed](/guides/self-employed-expense-tracking/).

## A worked report

An employee flies to Denver for a two-day client workshop, March 9 to 11, 2026, with a $200 advance:

| Date | Merchant | Category | Business purpose | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Feb 24 | Airline | Airfare | Round trip to Denver, client workshop | 286.40 |
| Mar 9 | Taxi | Ground transport | Airport to hotel | 42.00 |
| Mar 9–11 | Hotel | Lodging | 2 nights, workshop venue | 318.00 |
| Mar 10 | Restaurant | Meals | Dinner, traveling alone | 38.75 |
| Mar 11 | Taxi | Ground transport | Hotel to airport | 39.50 |
| Mar 11 | Airport parking | Parking | Home airport, 3 days | 48.00 |
| | | | **Total expenses** | **772.65** |
| | | | **Less advance** | **−200.00** |
| | | | **Due to employee** | **572.65** |

Things to notice:

- **The hotel needs its receipt at any amount.** Publication 463's $75 exception for documentary evidence excludes lodging. It says a hotel receipt is enough if it shows the hotel's name and location, the dates you stayed, and separate amounts for charges such as lodging, meals and telephone calls.
- **The $38.75 dinner is under $75,** so federal rules don't require a receipt for it, though the line still needs its date, place and purpose. Your employer's policy may still ask for one; follow the policy.
- **The airfare was paid on February 24.** The 60-day limit runs from when each expense was paid, so submitting by April 25, 60 days after February 24, keeps every line inside it.

For the travel rules behind each line, what counts as a business trip and what you can claim on one, see [business travel expenses](/guides/business-travel-expenses/).

## Before you submit

- Every line has a receipt, or a reason it doesn't (an expense under $75 other than lodging, or a transportation cost with no receipt readily available).
- Every receipt shows the merchant, date and amount you entered. Check the total against the receipt, not your memory.
- Personal charges on the same bill, like a hotel movie, are taken off.
- Advances are subtracted, and anything you owe back is ready to return.
- Keep your own copy of the report and the receipts. How long to keep them is covered in [which receipts to keep for taxes](/guides/which-receipts-to-keep-for-taxes/).
