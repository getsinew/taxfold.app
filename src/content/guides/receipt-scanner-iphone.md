An iPhone can scan receipts without any extra app. The harder part is making sure each copy is one you can still read and rely on months later, at tax time. This guide covers the built-in tools, a checklist for a scan worth keeping, long, faded and emailed receipts, and what the IRS says about electronic copies of paper records.

Some apps sold as receipt scanners are reward apps, where you scan shopping receipts to collect points. That is a different job. This guide is about keeping a usable record of what you spent.

## Three built-in ways to capture a receipt

### The document scanner in Notes

Apple's support page [How to scan documents on your iPhone or iPad](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108963) gives these steps:

1. Open Notes and select a note or create a new one.
2. Tap the Attachment button, then tap **Scan Documents**.
3. Place the receipt in view of the camera. Apple notes that in iOS 26 you can also choose options such as flash or an image filter.
4. In Auto mode the scan happens automatically. To capture it yourself, tap the Shutter button or press a volume button, then drag the corners to fit the page and tap **Keep Scan**.
5. Tap **Done**, or add more scans to the same document.

The last step matters for receipts: you can keep several scans together in one document, which is how you handle a receipt too long to fit in one frame (more on that below).

### Scanning straight into Files

The [same Apple page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108963) describes scanning from the Files app: tap the More button, tap **Scan Documents**, capture the page the same way, then choose a location and tap **Save**. Use this if you already keep receipts in folders, for example one folder per year.

### Live Text, for copying what the receipt says

Live Text reads text rather than storing a copy. Apple's page on [using Live Text with the iPhone camera](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-live-text-iphcf0b71b0e/ios) explains that you point the camera at text, wait for the yellow frame around the detected text, tap the button that appears, then touch and hold to select text and copy, share, look up or translate it. Live Text also works on photos you already have: Apple's page on [interacting with content in a photo](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/interact-with-content-in-a-photo-or-video-iph37fdd714b/ios) says it can be turned on in Settings > General > Language & Region, and it lists Photos, Camera, Safari and Quick Look among the places it works.

That makes Live Text handy for pulling a merchant name or a total into a spreadsheet or a note. It is not a record by itself. Apple also notes that Live Text isn't available in all regions or languages.

### Photo or scan: a decision rule

Both give you an image. Use the document scanner when you can, because it lets you crop the scan to the edges of the paper. Use an ordinary photo when the scanner won't detect the edges (a crumpled receipt, a receipt on a busy background), and crop it afterward. Whichever you use, apply the checklist below before you file it.

## What makes a scan worth keeping

The IRS page [What kind of records should I keep](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/what-kind-of-records-should-i-keep) says supporting documents for business expenses "should identify the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date incurred, and include a description of the item purchased or service received that shows the amount was for a business expense." It adds that "a combination of supporting documents may be needed." That gives you a practical test for each scan (this is US federal guidance):

- **Merchant name** is readable.
- **Date** is readable.
- **Total** is readable, along with any tax line.
- **What you bought** shows, either as item lines or as a note you add.
- **Payment** shows (card ending, "cash", "paid"), or you have a card statement or bank record that shows it.
- **The whole receipt** is in frame: top edge to bottom edge, nothing folded under.
- **Zoom check**: enlarge the scan and read the smallest numbers. If you hesitate over a digit, scan again.

The zoom check is not a house rule. It comes from the IRS standard for electronic records, covered further down, which defines legibility as being able to "identify all letters and numerals positively and quickly."

### Fixes for receipts that scan badly

| Problem | What to do |
|---|---|
| Receipt longer than the frame | Scan it in overlapping sections and add each one to the same Notes document |
| Glare on glossy or thermal paper | Turn off the flash, tilt the phone slightly, or move away from overhead light |
| Crumpled or curled | Flatten it under something heavy for a minute, then scan against a plain, darker surface |
| Print already faint | Scan it now, check it with the zoom test, and keep the paper if the scan fails |
| Card slip only, no item list | Keep the slip and add a note saying what was bought; keep the invoice if there is one |
| Two-sided (returns, warranty terms) | Scan both sides into the same document |

## Faded thermal paper

Many register receipts are printed on heat-sensitive paper, and the print can fade over time. The defense is timing: scan receipts the same day or the same week, not at the end of the year.

If a receipt has already faded past the zoom test, a scan won't rescue it. The IRS revenue procedure on electronic records, discussed below, lists "retaining hardcopies of books or records that are illegible or that cannot be accurately or completely transferred" among its recommended practices. So keep that paper, and back it up with whatever else you have: the card statement, an order confirmation, or a duplicate receipt from the merchant if they can print one.

## Emailed and digital receipts

A receipt that arrives by email or as a PDF from a store's website is already digital, so there is nothing to scan. The risk with these is scatter: some live in one inbox, some in another, some in a shop's account page. Pick one place and save them there, next to your scans, with the same folder scheme.

## What the IRS says about scanned copies (US federal)

Two primary sources cover this.

[Publication 583, Starting a Business and Keeping Records](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p583), says "all requirements that apply to hard copy books and records also apply to electronic storage systems that maintain tax books and records," and defines an electronic storage system as "any system for preparing or keeping your records either by electronic imaging or by transfer to an electronic storage media."

For the details, Publication 583 points to [Rev. Proc. 97-22 (Internal Revenue Bulletin 1997-13, from page 9)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-irbs/irb97-13.pdf). The points that matter for receipts:

- **It accepts electronic records that meet its terms.** Records kept in a system that "complies with the requirements of this revenue procedure will constitute records" under section 6001 of the Code.
- **The copy must be accurate and complete.** The system "must ensure an accurate and complete transfer" and must "index, store, preserve, retrieve, and reproduce" the records.
- **Legibility is defined.** The observer "must be able to identify all letters and numerals positively and quickly to the exclusion of all other letters or numerals."
- **The index can be simple.** The indexing requirement is met "if the indexing system is functionally comparable to a reasonable hardcopy filing system."
- **Backups are listed as a recommended practice**, along with keeping hardcopies of anything that can't be transferred legibly.
- **Throwing away paper has conditions.** It permits destroying the original hardcopy after the taxpayer has tested the system to confirm records are reproduced in compliance and "has instituted procedures that ensure its continued compliance."
- **Losing access counts as losing the records.** Records in a system whose hardware and software you stop maintaining "will be deemed destroyed," unless they stay available.
- **A provider doesn't take on your responsibility.** Using a third party for the system "does not relieve the taxpayer" of these responsibilities.

The revenue procedure sets requirements for a system. It does not list approved apps or devices. Two practical consequences follow from the last two points: when you replace your phone or delete an app, make sure the receipts come with you or exist as an export somewhere else, and keep a backup.

How long to keep the copies depends on the return they support. The IRS page [How long should I keep records?](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records) says you generally keep records that support an item on your return "until the period of limitations for that tax return runs out," and lists the periods, which run longer than the basic one in some situations. Our guide on [which receipts to keep for taxes](/guides/which-receipts-to-keep-for-taxes/) goes through them. State rules vary.

## Worked example: one week of receipts

A freelance designer comes home with three receipts and gets one by email.

1. **Printer ink, thermal register receipt, paid by card.** Scanned in Notes that evening. The zoom check passes. The receipt shows the card's last four digits, so payment is covered. She adds a line: "ink, studio printer."
2. **Hardware store, receipt about as long as her forearm.** Scanned in three overlapping sections into one Notes document. She checks the top section shows the store and date and the bottom shows the total.
3. **Parking, small card slip with no description.** Scanned, with a note: "client site visit, Tuesday."
4. **Software subscription, emailed PDF.** Saved to the same year folder as the scans. No scan needed.

At month end she copies the four totals into her spreadsheet with Live Text instead of retyping them. She files by year, then month, as our guide on [how to organize receipts](/guides/how-to-organize-receipts/) describes in more detail.

## What a dedicated receipt app adds

The built-in tools give you images. A receipt app turns each image into a record: it reads the date, merchant, total and tax lines into fields you can correct, files the receipt under a year and category, and lets you export a year at once instead of collecting files by hand. Text recognition does misread faint digits, so check the total against the image before you rely on it. An export also covers the "deemed destroyed" point above, because the export can live somewhere other than the phone.
